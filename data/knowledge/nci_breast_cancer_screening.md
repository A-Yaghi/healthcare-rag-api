Breast cancer screening looks for breast cancer in people who do not have symptoms. Finding breast cancer earlier may make it easier to treat. Screening has been found to reduce deaths from breast cancer and is an important part of routine health care for women. A doctor does not necessarily think someone has cancer when recommending a screening test.

Several tests may be used. Mammography is the standard screening test for most women. Breast MRI uses radio waves and a strong magnet without ionizing radiation. People at high risk may have breast MRI along with mammography because MRI is more sensitive and mammography alone may miss some cancers in high-risk women. People with dense breasts may be offered MRI with mammography, but it is not known whether supplemental MRI improves health outcomes in dense breasts.

Breast ultrasound uses high-energy sound waves. It may be offered with mammography for dense breasts, but it is not known whether additional ultrasound improves outcomes. A clinical breast exam alone is not an adequate screening test. Breast self-exams are also not adequate screening on their own, though people should tell a doctor about unusual changes even after a normal mammogram.

Benefits of screening include finding cancer before symptoms so treatment can start earlier and potentially be more effective. Potential harms include false-positive results that cause distress and additional procedures such as biopsies; overdiagnosis and overtreatment of cancers that would not have caused problems during a person's lifetime; delayed diagnosis from false-negative results, which are more common with dense breasts; delayed care if symptoms after a normal screen are ignored; and very low but nonzero radiation risk from mammography.

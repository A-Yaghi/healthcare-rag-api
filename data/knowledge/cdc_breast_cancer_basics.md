Breast cancer is a disease in which cells in the breast grow out of control. There are different kinds of breast cancer. A breast is made up of three main parts: lobules, ducts, and connective tissue. The lobules are glands that produce milk. The ducts are tubes that carry milk to the nipple. Connective tissue surrounds and holds everything together.

Most breast cancers begin in the ducts or lobules. When breast cancer spreads to other parts of the body, it is called metastatic breast cancer.

The most common types are invasive ductal carcinoma and invasive lobular carcinoma. Invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) accounts for about 70% to 80% of breast cancers. Cancer cells start in the ducts and spread to other parts of the breast and potentially through lymph nodes or bloodstream. A person with IDC may feel a lump in the breast or underarm, or have no symptoms with a small lump seen on a mammogram. Other symptoms can include breast pain, change in breast shape, or nipple discharge.

Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) accounts for about 10% to 15% of breast cancers. Cancer cells begin in the lobules and spread to nearby tissues. ILC often does not make a classic lump and may appear as thickened connective tissue, making it harder to see on a mammogram. ILC is more likely than IDC to form in both breasts.

Breast cancers are divided into subtypes based on estrogen and progesterone hormone receptors and how much HER2 protein they make. These markers help guide treatment. Triple-negative breast cancer does not have estrogen or progesterone receptors and does not make HER2 protein, so there are fewer targeted treatment options. It makes up about 15% of breast cancers, is often diagnosed later, grows more quickly, and is more likely to come back. It is more often diagnosed at a younger age in Black women than in White women and is more common with certain gene changes, including BRCA1 or BRCA2.

Inflammatory breast cancer is a type of IDC affecting breast skin; blocked lymph vessels make the breast warm, red, and swollen, sometimes with orange-peel dimpling and an inverted nipple. It grows and spreads faster than many other types. Paget's disease of the breast is a rare cancer of the nipple and areola that can look like eczema; most people with it also have tumors in the same breast.

People with a strong family history may have gene changes that increase risk. Doctors may recommend genetic counseling and possibly genetic testing to help manage risk.

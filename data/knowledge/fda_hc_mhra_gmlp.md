The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), Health Canada, and the United Kingdom's Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) jointly identified 10 guiding principles that can inform the development of Good Machine Learning Practice (GMLP). These principles help promote safe, effective, and high-quality medical devices that use artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML).

1. Multi-Disciplinary Expertise Is Leveraged Throughout the Total Product Life Cycle: In-depth understanding of a model's intended integration into clinical workflow, and the desired benefits and associated patient risks, can help ensure that ML-enabled medical devices are safe and effective and address clinically meaningful needs over the lifecycle of the device.

2. Good Software Engineering and Security Practices Are Implemented: Model design should use good software engineering practices, data quality assurance, data management, and robust cybersecurity practices, including methodical risk management and documentation of design and risk decisions.

3. Clinical Study Participants and Data Sets Are Representative of the Intended Patient Population: Relevant characteristics of the intended patient population (for example age, gender, sex, race, and ethnicity), use, and measurement inputs should be sufficiently represented so results can be reasonably generalized and bias can be managed.

4. Training Data Sets Are Independent of Test Sets: Training and test datasets should be appropriately independent. Potential sources of dependence, including patient, data acquisition, and site factors, should be considered and addressed.

5. Selected Reference Datasets Are Based Upon Best Available Methods: Reference standards should use accepted, best available methods so clinically relevant and well characterized data are collected and limitations of the reference are understood.

6. Model Design Is Tailored to the Available Data and Reflects the Intended Use of the Device: Design should mitigate known risks such as overfitting, performance degradation, and security risks, and support clinically meaningful performance goals tied to intended use.

7. Focus Is Placed on the Performance of the Human-AI Team: Where there is a human in the loop, human factors and interpretability should emphasize Human-AI team performance rather than model performance alone.

8. Testing Demonstrates Device Performance During Clinically Relevant Conditions: Statistically sound test plans should generate clinically relevant performance information independently of the training set, considering population subgroups, clinical environment, inputs, and confounding factors.

9. Users Are Provided Clear, Essential Information: Users should receive clear information on intended use, subgroup performance, training and test data characteristics, acceptable inputs, known limitations, UI interpretation, workflow integration, updates, and how to communicate concerns.

10. Deployed Models Are Monitored for Performance and Re-training Risks Are Managed: Deployed models should be monitored in real-world use. When models are retrained after deployment, controls should manage overfitting, unintended bias, and degradation such as dataset drift.

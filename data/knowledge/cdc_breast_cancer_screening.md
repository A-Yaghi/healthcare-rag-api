Breast cancer screening means checking a woman's breasts for cancer before there are signs or symptoms. Screening cannot prevent breast cancer, but it can help find breast cancer early, when it is easier to treat. Informed and shared decision-making means learning the benefits and risks of screening and deciding with a doctor whether screening is right and when to have it.

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends that women who are 40 to 74 years old and at average risk for breast cancer get a mammogram every 2 years. Different recommendations may apply for women at higher than average risk.

A mammogram is an x-ray of the breast. For many women, mammograms are the best way to find breast cancer early. Getting mammograms on a regular schedule can lower the risk of dying from breast cancer. At this time, a mammogram is the best way to find breast cancer for most women of screening age.

Breast MRI uses magnets and radio waves and is used along with mammograms to screen women at high risk. Because breast MRIs may appear abnormal even when there is no cancer, they are not used alone for average-risk screening. Breast ultrasound uses sound waves to make pictures of areas inside the breast. Clinical breast exams and breast self-awareness can help notice changes, but they are not adequate screening tests on their own.

Benefit of screening includes finding cancer early, when the tumor has not spread outside the breast. CDC notes that more than 99% of women whose breast cancer is found at an early localized stage are alive 5 years after diagnosis.

Risks include false-positive results that lead to more tests, expense, invasive procedures, and anxiety; overdiagnosis and overtreatment of cancers that may never have caused problems; false-negative results that can delay diagnosis; pain during procedures; and radiation exposure from mammography. While the radiation dose is small, repeated x-rays may carry some risk.

Screening can be done at clinics, hospitals, or doctor's offices. Most health insurance plans are required to cover screening mammograms every 1 to 2 years for women beginning at age 40 with no out-of-pocket cost. CDC's National Breast and Cervical Cancer Early Detection Program offers free or low-cost mammograms for eligible people.

Mammography is an imaging test that uses low-dose x-rays to create pictures of the breast. It is used both to screen for breast cancer and to diagnose breast conditions. Screening mammograms can find tumors at an earlier stage, before they cause symptoms. Diagnostic mammograms check for breast cancer after a person or clinician finds a lump or other change and usually require images from more angles, so the radiation dose is higher.

Mammograms can show a mass (breast lump), deposits of calcium called calcifications, and other changes. They also show breast density. When possible, a radiologist compares the newest mammogram with earlier ones to check for changes over time.

Masses that look smooth, round, and well defined are often benign cysts, while masses with jagged outlines or irregular shapes may need more testing. Macrocalcifications look like small white dots and are usually benign. Microcalcifications look like white specks; if grouped in certain ways or found in rapidly dividing cells, they may be a sign of DCIS or breast cancer. Mammography is more likely to miss cancer in women with dense breasts.

Screening results are often reported with BI-RADS categories: 0 needs additional imaging; 1 is negative; 2 is benign; 3 is probably benign with short-interval follow-up; 4 is suspicious and may require biopsy; 5 is highly suggestive of malignancy; and 6 is known biopsy-proven malignancy.

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force currently recommends that women at average risk of breast cancer have screening mammograms every 2 years between ages 40 and 74. People at very high risk may need earlier or more frequent screening. Three-dimensional mammography, also called digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT), creates 3D images from multiple thin slices and can be better at finding tumors than standard mammography alone; research continues on whether it reduces deaths more effectively.

Women with breast implants should continue getting mammograms, and the facility should be told about implants because implant displacement views may be needed. In the United States, the Mammography Quality Standards Act (MQSA) requires mammography facilities to meet uniform quality standards and provide easy-to-read result reports.

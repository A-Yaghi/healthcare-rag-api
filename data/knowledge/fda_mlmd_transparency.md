Transparency for machine learning-enabled medical devices (MLMDs) describes the degree to which appropriate information about a device, including its intended use, development, performance and, when available, logic, is clearly communicated to relevant audiences. Logic refers to information about how an output or result was reached. Explainability is the degree to which that logic can be understood by a person.

These transparency principles build on Good Machine Learning Practice (GMLP), especially the principles that focus on Human-AI team performance and that users are provided clear, essential information. Effective transparency ensures that information that could impact risks and patient outcomes is communicated; considers what intended users need and the context of use; uses suitable media, timing, and strategies; and relies on a holistic understanding of users, environments, and workflows.

Relevant audiences include people who use the device (such as health care professionals, patients, and caregivers), people who receive care with the device, and additional parties who make decisions about the device to support patient outcomes (such as support staff, administrators, payors, and governing bodies).

Transparency supports safe and effective use, patient-centered care, informed decision-making, detection of errors or performance decline, health equity through identification of bias, and trust and adoption of beneficial technologies. A clear intended use helps people understand whether a device is intended to inform or replace the judgment of a health care provider.

Relevant information often includes medical purpose and function; diseases or conditions addressed; intended users, environments, and target populations; how the device fits into workflow; intended inputs and outputs; performance, benefits, and risks; bias management; model logic when available and understandable; training and testing data characterization; clinical study summaries; known limitations, failure modes, confidence intervals, and underrepresented populations; and how safety and effectiveness are maintained through monitoring and change management.

Information can be placed in the user interface, including training materials, controls, displays, packaging, labelling, and alarms. Timing matters across the total product lifecycle, including acquisition decisions, use decisions, update notifications, and targeted warnings during high-risk workflow steps. Human-centered design helps make transparency usable by iterating with relevant parties and matching language and detail to the audience.

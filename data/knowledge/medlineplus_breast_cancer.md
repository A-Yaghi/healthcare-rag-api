Breast cancer is a cancer that starts in breast tissue. It happens when cells in the breast change and grow out of control. New cells grow even when they are not needed, and old cells do not die when they should. These extra cells can form a mass called a tumor.

Sometimes the cancer does not spread further. This is called "in situ." If the cancer spreads outside the breast, it is called invasive. It may spread to nearby tissues and lymph nodes, or metastasize to other parts of the body through the lymph system or the blood. Breast cancer is the second most common type of cancer in women in the United States. Rarely, it can also affect men.

Types are based on which breast cells turn into cancer. Ductal carcinoma begins in the cells of the ducts and is the most common type. Lobular carcinoma begins in the lobules and is more often found in both breasts than other types. Inflammatory breast cancer is rare; cancer cells block lymph vessels in the skin of the breast, making the breast warm, red, and swollen. Paget's disease of the breast involves the skin of the nipple and is also rare.

Breast cancer happens when there are changes in DNA. The exact cause is often unknown. Some genetic changes are inherited (hereditary breast cancer). Changes in BRCA1 and BRCA2 can raise risk of breast and ovarian cancers. Lifestyle and environment can also affect risk.

Risk factors include older age; history of breast cancer or benign breast disease; inherited risk including BRCA1/BRCA2 changes; dense breast tissue; reproductive history with more estrogen exposure (early menstruation, older age at first birth or never giving birth, later menopause, menopausal hormone therapy); radiation therapy to the breast or chest; obesity; and drinking alcohol.

Signs and symptoms can include a new lump or thickening in or near the breast or armpit; change in breast size or shape; dimple or puckering of breast skin; nipple turned inward; nipple discharge other than breast milk; scaly, red, or swollen skin in the nipple area or breast; and pain in any area of the breast.

Diagnosis may use a physical exam including clinical breast exam, medical history, imaging such as mammogram, ultrasound, or MRI, breast biopsy, and blood chemistry tests. Cancer-cell tests may include genetic tests, HER2 testing, and estrogen/progesterone receptor testing. Staging determines whether cancer has spread and may include imaging and sentinel lymph node biopsy.

Treatments can include mastectomy or lumpectomy, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Prevention strategies discussed by MedlinePlus include healthy weight, limiting alcohol, exercise, limiting estrogen exposure where relevant, medicines or preventive surgery for some high-risk people, and regular mammograms for earlier detection.

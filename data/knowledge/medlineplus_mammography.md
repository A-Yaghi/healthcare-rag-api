A mammogram is an x-ray picture of the breast. Health care providers use mammograms to look for early signs of breast cancer that can't be felt during a breast exam. There are two types of mammograms: screening mammograms and diagnostic mammograms.

A screening mammogram is usually done for women who have no signs or symptoms of breast cancer. Regular screening mammograms can help reduce the number of deaths from breast cancer among women ages 40 to 74 because they can find breast cancer early and treatment can start earlier, maybe before it has spread.

Screening mammograms can also have risks. They can sometimes find something that looks abnormal but isn't cancer. This leads to further testing and can cause anxiety. Sometimes mammograms can miss cancer when it is there. Mammograms also expose people to radiation. Patients should talk with their provider about benefits and drawbacks, including when to start and how often to have a mammogram.

A diagnostic mammogram takes longer than a screening mammogram. It is done for people who have a lump or other signs or symptoms of breast cancer, such as breast pain, thickening of the skin of the breast, nipple discharge, or a change in breast size or shape. These signs can also be caused by a benign (not cancer) breast condition. A diagnostic mammogram may also be used if someone has breast implants or if breast tissue is otherwise difficult to view.

During a mammogram, the breast is placed between two plastic plates that press and flatten the breast so a clear picture can be obtained. Both breasts are usually x-rayed from the front and from the side. A radiologist then reads the mammogram for early signs of breast cancer or other problems.

An abnormal mammogram does not always mean that there is cancer. Additional mammograms, tests, or exams are needed before a provider can tell for sure. Referral to a breast specialist or surgeon does not necessarily mean cancer or surgery is required; these clinicians are experts in diagnosing breast problems.
